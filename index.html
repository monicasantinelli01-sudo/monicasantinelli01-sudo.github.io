Prostitution in San Francisco

Prostitution as a crime category is analyzed in the area of San Francisco. The data sources are two official datasets from the San Francisco Police Department 1. One covers the time from 2003 to 2018 and the other covers the period from 2018 until the end of 2025. The datasets are merged and the focus is on how the crime patterns of Prostitution changed within the above-mentioned time period.

Spatial concentration

As shown in the heatmap, prostitution-related incidents are most concentrated in the districts Central, Northern, Southern, Tenderloin and Mission. The heatmap also reveals how these patterns have evolved over the years: the trends show a gradual reduction in the intensity of the hotspots, especially in the later years of the period.

District patterns

Yearly prostitution trends by police district

We will be looking more into details at these five districts. Following, an analysis of the reasons which could have led to the decrease of prostitution incidents and the shift of prostitution clusters towards the Mission District within that period.

Southern

The Southern district shows a moderate level of incidents for much of the period, followed by a sharp local peak in the mid-2010s and then a strong decline. Thinking of the social and economic factors, the district made a massive demographic and constructive change in the last decades. It went from an industrial environment with warehouses and plants to a global technology center. This led to a significant gentrification, increasing housing costs and the displacement of working-class citizens in that part of the town2,3. Moreover, the district did undergo a big change in terms of urban densification based on the Transit Center District Plan of 2012 which resulted in a higher portion of skyscrapers within the district4. In terms of criminality, mainly areas near the Market street, between the 5th and 10th street have a high crime concentration including prostitution, drugs and violence56.

Tenderloin

Since the early 2000s Tenderloin remained one of the most criminal districts in San Francisco. In contrast to other districts, gentrification did not take place as extensively. Tenderloin is also associated with poverty, homelessness, drug activity, and prostitution-related incidents789. As later mentioned also in the Northern district section, especially areas near Polk Street were likely to be involved in crimes linked to Prostitution. Moreover, the district is connected to acute poverty as a high portion of the citizens is living in single room occupancy hotels 10.

Central

The Central district saw major demographic change from 2000 to 2015, with rising living costs contributing to the displacement of lower-income residents11. Historically, prostitution activity was also pushed away from the central core toward nearby districts such as Tenderloin12. Thus, in the recent past, the main tasks of the police department these days are the management of parades and major events and managing property-related crimes like theft or burglary13.

Northern

The Northern district was one of the historical hotspots of prostitution-related incidents. Areas around Polk Street and nearby corridors were repeatedly associated with sex work in the first decade of the 2000s14. It is to be noted that Northern shares a border with the Tenderloin district mainly along Polk Street and Larkin Street. However the Northern district also experienced a big demographic and social shift. In the period 2000-2015, the district lost a high portion of low-income black households due to increasing housing prices15. The district also includes the area of the city called Fisherman’s Wharf, which is a popular tourist destination and has a high concentration of hotels, restaurants, and entertainment venues. This could be one of the reasons why prostitution-related activity in that district decreased over time, as the area became more commercialized and less accessible for street-based prostitution.

Mission

Mission shows remained one of the districts where prostitution-related activity continued to appear more persistently than in several other parts of the city. Recent reporting describes a shift of visible street prostitution within the district rather than a disappearance of the activity. After Capp Street was closed to through traffic in February 2023, nearby residents reported that “the same problems have instead moved to their doorsteps” and one resident said, “When the barriers went up … all the prostitution moved to Shotwell.”16. In response, the city announced additional barriers on Shotwell, visible public cameras, and the Dear John program, under which SFPD said it had begun sending letters and pictures to the addresses of registered vehicle owners seen and captured on camera soliciting prostitution along Shotwell and surrounding streets.17 The purpose was to discourage potential clients from going to the area and to reduce the demand for prostitution, but as said from residents, the effect was the opposite as the activity just shifted to nearby corridors and streets.18

The question is: what's the real story behind the decrease in prostitution in San Francisco?

What needs to be noted is that the decrease in prostitution-related incidents in San Francisco does not necessarily indicate a reduction in the actual prevalence of prostitution. The lower reported incidents may reflect changes in law enforcement priorities, reporting practices, or the displacement of prostitution to other areas or online platforms.

Shift toward online solicitation

Some of the long decline in reported street prostitution may also reflect a broader move toward online solicitation. PBS reported in 2010 that Craigslist removed its adult-services ads, and Cunningham and Kendall describe the rise of “institutions that facilitate prostitution online.” A 2011 TIME article likewise described sex workers shifting from Craigslist to Facebook. (Source?) Moreover, a report from KALW argues that San Francisco's tech boom and gentrification did not really reduce sex work but instead restructured it along class lines, pushing wealthier clients and workers to online platforms while leaving poorer individuals on the streets.

Programs and debate

During the investigated time period, various organizations and programs were trying to influence legislation, support sex workers, or prevent Johns from soliciting sex workers in the future. For instance, the US PROS Collective is still fighting for the decriminalization of prostitution and aims for protection, justice, and resources to prevent people from being forced into sex work due to poverty or violence. Moreover, programs like “John Schools” focus on preventing further contact after initial solicitation. Johns, after being convicted, have to join virtual courses providing information regarding potential health risks, sex trafficking education, and legal consequences. However, there was also criticism regarding these “John School” initiatives. For instance, Local and Jordan claimed that the decrease in prostitution incidents actually began two years before the program was implemented and that the study never measured actual participants. Therefore, the authors state that there is no actual evidence that these programs reduce re-offending rates.

The Whac-A-Mole Effect: displacement rather than disappearance

Recent reporting shows that street-level prostitution in the Mission was not simply removed, but pushed between nearby streets. Mission Local wrote that after Capp Street was closed to through traffic, “the same problems have instead moved to their doorsteps,” and NBC Bay Area reported that “the prostitution problem was pushed a few blocks down” to Shotwell Street. Later coverage in the San Francisco Standard said residents feared new barriers on Shotwell would “just move the problem” again, while Mission Local quoted a resident saying, “all it’s going to do is move the sex workers from this street over to some new street, just like it did from Capp.” This supports a measured reading of the data: lower counts in one corridor do not automatically mean the activity is gone, only that its visible geography may have changed.

Changes in policing strategy and priorities

There were various changes in policing strategies in the time period from 2017 to 2025. In 2017, the SFPD released a Department Bulletin which changed police behavior regarding prostitution incidents. In the document, it was stated that sex workers who are witnesses or victims of violent crimes like assault, trafficking, stalking, robbery, kidnapping, extortion, etc. cannot be arrested due to prostitution incidents. It was also stated that officers who misbehave in contact with sex workers will face disciplinary consequences. Another legal change was the Safer Streets Act from 2022 which changed behavior regarding loitering. In this senate bill it was stated that it is no longer possible to convict someone for being suspected of intent to engage in prostitution. Additionally, people previously convicted under the loitering law could now petition for dismissal and sealing of their records. Furthermore, any minors arrested for prostitution-related offenses were automatically presumed to be victims, not criminals. These legal adjustments influenced the crime statistics directly after their release. For instance, in the first half of 2023, solicitation charges against clients made up nearly 80% of all prostitution-related incidents.

Conclusion

The decline in prostitution-related incidents in San Francisco should be read cautiously. The data strongly suggest that visible street prostitution changed in form and geography rather than simply disappearing. Gentrification, policing changes, legal reforms, and the rise of online solicitation all seem to have reshaped where and how prostitution-related activity appears in official records. In some districts, declining counts may reflect displacement, reduced street visibility, or altered enforcement priorities rather than a true disappearance of the activity itself. Taken together, the pattern is less a clean story of reduction and more a story of transformation.

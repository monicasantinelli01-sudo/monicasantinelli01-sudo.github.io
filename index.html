Prostitution in San Francisco

The following analysis examines prostitution-related crime patterns in San Francisco, using two official datasets from the San Francisco Police Department1. One covers the time from 2003 to 2018 and the other covers the period from 2018 until the end of 2025. The datasets are merged and the focus is on how the crime patterns of Prostitution changed within the above-mentioned time period.

Spatial concentration

As shown in the heatmap, prostitution-related incidents are most concentrated in the districts Central, Northern, Southern, Tenderloin and Mission. The heatmap also reveals how these patterns have evolved over the years: the trends show a gradual reduction in the intensity of the hotspots, especially in the later years of the period.

District patterns

Yearly prostitution trends by police district

We will be looking more into details at these five districts. Following, an analysis of the reasons which could have led to the decrease of prostitution incidents and the shift of prostitution clusters towards the Mission District within that period.

Southern

The Southern District shows a moderate level of prostitution-related incidents for much of the period, followed by a sharp local peak in the mid-2010s and then a strong decline. Thinking of the social and economic factors, the district made a massive demographic and constructive change in the last decades. It went from an industrial environment with warehouses and plants to a global technology center. This led to a significant gentrification, increasing housing costs and the displacement of working-class citizens in that part of the town2,3. Moreover, the district did undergo a big change in terms of urban densification based on the Transit Center District Plan of 2012 which resulted in a higher portion of skyscrapers within the district4. In terms of criminality, mainly areas near the Market street, between the 5th and 10th street have a high crime concentration including prostitution, drugs and violence5,6.

Tenderloin

Since the early 2000s Tenderloin was one of the most criminal districts in San Francisco. In contrast to other districts, this neighborhood has resisted or complicated typical gentrification patterns over time. A 2015 analysis argues it has been relatively “immune” to gentrification because a large share of housing is permanently affordable or nonprofit-controlled, limiting speculation and displacement even as nearby areas upscale7. Years later, coverage emphasizes that instead of gentrifying, the neighborhood has increasingly concentrated poverty, homelessness and drug activity, with open-air drug markets and social crises dominating daily life, leading to —so much so that children need escorts to safely navigate the streets— suggesting that Tenderloin is still one of San Francisco’s most troubled areas despite broader citywide development pressures8,9. All this confirms the fact that the district has been historically associated with prostitution-related activity and it can still be considered one of the areas with the highest concentration of prostitution-related incidents in the city, even if the trends show a decrease in the number of incidents over time. Moreover, as also later mentioned in the Northern district section, the hotspots are located along the border along Polk Street and Larkin Street shared by Tenderloin and Northern districts, thus it can be hard to distinguish the two districts in terms of prostitution-related activity.

Central

The Central District experienced significant demographic change between 2000 and 2015, as rising housing costs contributed to the displacement of lower-income residents across San Francisco2. Historically, prostitution activity in the city has been more strongly associated with nearby areas such as the Tenderloin, which has long been recognized as a center of vice-related activity10. As a result, the Central District does not emerge as a primary long-term prostitution hotspot in the data. Instead, it functions as a high-demand downtown policing area: it accounted for a substantial share of citywide calls for service and led in several categories, including total incidents and property crimes such as larceny/theft and car break-ins11. This suggests that policing priorities in the district are more focused on managing tourism, major events, and property-related crime11, which may help explain the relatively lower and declining levels of prostitution-related incidents observed over time.

Northern

The Northern District was historically a hotspot for prostitution-related incidents, particularly along Polk Street and nearby corridors, which were closely linked to street-based sex work in the early 2000s12. This area borders the Tenderloin, especially along Polk and Larkin Streets, where such activity has also been concentrated, creating a contiguous zone of high activity across district boundaries. Thus the two districts can be considered as part of a single area with high prostitution-related activity. Over time, however, the Northern District experienced significant demographic and economic changes. Between 2000 and 2015, rising housing costs contributed to the displacement of many low-income residents, including a substantial decline in Black households across central San Francisco neighborhoods2. At the same time, parts of the district—especially around Polk Street and Fisherman’s Wharf—became increasingly commercialized, with growth in tourism, nightlife, and higher-end businesses13. These shifts likely reduced the viability of street-based prostitution in the area. Increased commercialization, changing land use, and demographic turnover may have made the district less accessible or less suitable for such activity, contributing to the steady decline in prostitution-related incidents observed in the data.

Mission

The Mission District remained one of the areas where prostitution-related activity persisted more consistently than in several other parts of the city. However, recent reporting suggests that declines in recorded incidents may reflect spatial displacement rather than a true reduction in activity. For example, after Capp Street was closed to through traffic in early 2023, residents reported that street-based prostitution shifted to nearby corridors such as Shotwell Street, with one noting that “when the barriers went up … all the prostitution moved to Shotwell”1415. In response, city authorities introduced additional measures, including traffic barriers, public surveillance cameras, and the “Dear John” program, which targets demand by notifying individuals identified as soliciting prostitution16. While these interventions aim to deter activity, local accounts indicate that they may have primarily redistributed it within the district rather than eliminated it14. This pattern of displacement helps explain why prostitution-related incidents in the Mission decline over time in the data, yet remain present at non-negligible levels.

The question is: what's the real story behind the decrease in prostitution in San Francisco?

What needs to be noted is that the decrease in prostitution-related incidents in San Francisco does not necessarily indicate a reduction in the actual prevalence of prostitution. The lower reported incidents may reflect changes in law enforcement priorities, reporting practices, or the displacement of prostitution to other areas or online platforms.

Shift toward online solicitation

Part of the long-term decline in reported street prostitution may reflect a broader shift toward online solicitation rather than a true reduction in activity. In 2010, Craigslist removed its “adult services” section under public pressure, prompting significant changes in how sex work was advertised and arranged17. Researchers Cunningham and Kendall describe the emergence of “institutions that facilitate prostitution online,” highlighting how digital platforms increasingly replaced street-based markets. At the same time, sex workers adapted by moving to other online spaces. A 2011 TIME article18 reported that many individuals shifted from Craigslist to platforms such as Facebook, illustrating the resilience and adaptability of the market. In San Francisco specifically, reporting from KALW suggests that the tech boom and gentrification did not eliminate sex work but instead restructured it along class lines, with wealthier clients and workers moving to more discreet online arrangements while more vulnerable populations remained in street-based environments19. This transition helps explain why prostitution-related incidents decline in official data: as activity moves online and indoors, it becomes less visible to law enforcement and less likely to be captured in street-level incident reports.

Programs and debate

During the period studied, various organizations and policies thought to reshape how prostitution was addressed, focusing both on supporting sex workers and reducing demand. For example, advocacy groups such as US PROStitutes Collective promote the decriminalization of sex work and emphasize access to resources, protection, and alternatives for individuals driven into the industry by poverty or violence20. At the same time, law enforcement also introduced targeted demand strategies such as “John Schools”, which require individuals arrested for solicitation to attend educational sessions on legal consequences, health risks, and issues such as trafficking22. These programs are designed to reduce repeat offenses by discouraging future engagement with street-based prostitution. However, their effectiveness is debated. Research by Rachel Lovell and Ann Jordan found limited evidence of reduced reoffending, and some reporting suggests that declines in prostitution-related incidents began before such programs were implemented21,22. Overall, while these interventions may have influenced enforcement practices, the available evidence suggests they are unlikely to fully explain the long-term decline in prostitution-related incidents.

The Whac-A-Mole Effect: displacement rather than disappearance

Recent reporting suggests that street-level prostitution in the Mission has not been eliminated but rather displaced between nearby streets. Coverage by Mission Local describes how, after Capp Street was closed to through traffic, “the same problems have instead moved to their doorsteps,” with activity shifting to nearby corridors such as Shotwell Street14. Residents and local reporting consistently frame these interventions as temporary fixes that redistribute, rather than resolve, the issue. As one resident noted, “all it’s going to do is move the sex workers from this street over to some new street, just like it did from Capp”14. This supports a cautious interpretation of the data: declines in prostitution-related incidents in specific locations do not necessarily indicate an overall reduction in activity, but may instead reflect changes in its spatial distribution.

Changes in policing and legal framework

The decline in prostitution-related incidents between 2017 and 2025 can be linked in part to changes in both policing practices and the legal framework. A key shift occurred in 2017, when the San Francisco Police Department issued a bulletin prioritizing the safety of sex workers. The directive stated that individuals engaged in sex work who are victims or witnesses of violent crimes should not be arrested for prostitution-related offenses, and it introduced disciplinary consequences for officers who engage in misconduct23. In addition to changes in policing strategy, legal reforms at the state level significantly altered how prostitution-related activity is recorded. Prior to 2023, police could arrest individuals under California’s anti-loitering law, which accounted for a large share of prostitution-related citations. According to reporting by Local News Matters, such arrests represented around 65% of all sex work-related citations in recent years22. This changed with the passage of Senate Bill 357 (the Safer Streets Act), which decriminalized loitering for the purpose of engaging in prostitution and allowed individuals with prior convictions to seek dismissal of their records24. As a result, law enforcement lost a major tool for making prostitution-related arrests. The impact of these legal changes is visible in the data. In early 2023, solicitation charges against clients accounted for nearly 80% of prostitution-related incidents, indicating a shift away from policing sex workers themselves toward targeting demand22. This suggests that the observed decline in incidents reflects not only behavioral changes, but also a redefinition of what is recorded as a prostitution-related offense.

The shift in policing priorities is also reflected in the plot on the left, which is interactive, allowing users to toggle individual police districts to explore trends more clearly. It shows arrest rates for prostitution incidents across police districts. In the early 2000s, arrest rates for prostitution-related incidents were consistently high across all five districts, often close to 90–100%, suggesting that most reported incidents resulted in an arrest. From around 2012–2015 onwards, however, these rates began to decline notably across multiple districts, such as Tenderloin, indicating that officers were increasingly responding to incidents without making arrests. This pattern aligns with the broader shift in policing strategies described above. The Mission district, however, consistently shows both relatively high incident counts and high arrest rates, suggesting it remained a key hotspot receiving sustained police attention. Fluctuations in the data from around 2018 onwards should be interpreted with caution. As total incident counts declined, the number of cases per district became very small, meaning that even a single arrest could significantly affect the percentage.

The extreme swings between 0% and 100% observed in some districts in recent years should be interpreted with caution, as very small sample sizes can produce unstable percentages. Rather than treating these fluctuations as evidence of a behavioral shift, they are better understood as raising further questions. For example, are these late-period patterns primarily statistical artifacts, or do they also reflect changes in enforcement priorities, reporting practices, or the visibility of prostitution-related activity?

Beyond the decline

The decline in prostitution-related incidents in San Francisco should be read cautiously. The data strongly suggest that visible street prostitution changed in form and geography rather than simply disappearing. Gentrification, policing changes, legal reforms, and the rise of online solicitation all seem to have reshaped where and how prostitution-related activity appears in official records. In some districts, declining counts may reflect displacement, reduced street visibility, or altered enforcement priorities rather than a true disappearance of the activity itself. Taken together, the pattern is less a clean story of reduction and more a story of transformation.
